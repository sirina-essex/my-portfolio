The Inappropriate Use of Surveys: The Cambridge Analytica Case

The Cambridge Analytica affair is not an isolated ethical lapse. It is a concrete demonstration of what happens when a digital system is designed without effective governance, without traceability of use, and without any post-collection data security. What the public perceived as a political scandal or a moral failing is, in reality, a structural compromise of the digital interaction model, where the fundamental principles of cybersecurity were ignored from the outset.

Background

In 2014, the Facebook app This Is Your Digital Life enabled a third-party company to harvest the personal data of over 50 million users through default permissions and an open API architecture. None of this data was encrypted, monitored, or even limited in duration or purpose. Hoffman (2022) characterises this mass extraction as an inherent drift of permissive digital architectures, emphasising that the deliberate absence of encryption and oversight constitutes, in itself, a flagrant breach of the security and minimisation requirements imposed by the GDPR (Articles 5 and 32). This was not a traditional attack but an operational hijacking enabled by the lack of technical governance.

A New Threat Model

The compromise unfolded as a coherent sequence: an enticing interface, a permissive authorisation model, unsupervised extraction, opaque algorithmic processing, and invisible behavioural targeting. Through this process, the data was transformed into exploitable psychometric profiles via the OCEAN model, allowing segments of the population to be targeted according to their cognitive vulnerabilities. Kosinski, Stillwell and Graepel (2013) demonstrated precisely how this psychometric model became a formidable weapon because of its capacity to infer intimate traits from simple digital interactions, which could then be exploited for political manipulation. The objective: to influence their political perceptions without them ever perceiving the mechanism. This is no longer advertising-it is large-scale social engineering.

This case inaugurates a threat model that classical cybersecurity still does not fully incorporate: there is no malware, no intrusion, no malicious code. Yet the effect is identical to that of a successful attack: loss of confidentiality, loss of integrity, and loss of control over information systems-except here, the system is the individual. The user becomes in turn the vector (data provider), the target (object of targeting), and the victim (of directed manipulations). Zuboff (2019) clearly conceptualises this exploitation as the systematic extraction of 'behavioural surplus', turning the individual not only into a passive data source but also into the active target of a fundamentally intrusive and manipulative economic model.

Constrained Consent

The GDPR, conceived retrospectively and intended to protect against such abuses, relies on free and informed consent. But in practice, these protections are undermined by what is known as constrained consent: the user accepts the conditions for lack of any real alternative, without reading or understanding the standardised terms, which are incomprehensible and embedded within the very architecture of the platforms. Solove (2021) explicitly criticises this drift, pointing out that consent-supposedly protective under Article 7 of the GDPR becomes, in reality, a legal fiction due to its forced integration into complex digital environments, an analysis confirmed by European case law (CJEU, Planet49, 2019). The legal system protects the form of consent but guarantees neither its quality nor its real impact on practices. The asymmetry between the user and the platform remains intact.

Biometric Data in the FaceApp Case

The FaceApp affair, which occurred in 2019, replicated the same mechanism. This application offered visual filters in exchange for access to users’ photos, which it transferred to servers outside the EU, without governance, without auditability, and without any clear purpose. O’Flaherty (2019) specifically warned about the critical risks associated with the uncontrolled transfer of sensitive biometric data to foreign jurisdictions, a situation exacerbated by the absence of a robust international regulatory framework, as highlighted by Greenleaf (2018), in direct violation of Article 9 of the GDPR. Here, the data is no longer behavioural but biometric. Once captured, it becomes irrevocable, permanent, and usable for training facial recognition models, without any user being able to request its genuine deletion. This is a technical no-man’s-land where biometrics become an exploitable asset without governance or accountability.

Systemic Opacity and Architectural Responsibility

In both cases, the problem is not legal: it is architectural. These systems are designed to extract, aggregate, cross-reference and derive, without any concern for transparency or traceability. The resulting algorithmic models are invisible, inaccessible and immutable. The user can neither know what the platforms infer about them, nor request corrections, nor oppose their use. Pasquale (2015) identifies this phenomenon as systemic opacity inherent in the current digital economic model, which directly undermines individuals’ fundamental right-explicitly guaranteed under Article 22 of the GDPR, to understand and challenge automated processing that targets them. The individual is embedded in a system that uses them but that they can never interrogate. This amounts to a total absence of effective governance in a system where the declared purposes are merely contractual pretexts.

Cybersecurity as a Governance Function

Cybersecurity can no longer be limited to protecting technical infrastructures. It must become a core professional standpoint at the intersection of ethics, law, and structural responsibility. Henderson (2024) advocates precisely for this, asserting that cybersecurity must evolve to become a true governance discipline, explicitly incorporating ethical and legal responsibility into the very design of digital systems. It is a discipline of governance, centred on system design, use limitation, and permanent auditability. The cybersecurity professional no longer merely protects access; they become the guarantor of processing integrity, algorithmic accountability and the legitimacy of models. This requires a clear repositioning of their role-not as a passive gatekeeper, but as a structuring actor in digital architecture.

Conclusion

Cambridge Analytica is not an anomaly. It is a consequence. The economic model of major platforms is based on maximal capture, permanent reuse and systemic opacity. This economic dynamic, described by Zuboff (2019) under the concept of 'surveillance capitalism', is the structural root of scandals such as Cambridge Analytica, rendering traditional regulation largely ineffective as long as this logic remains unchanged. Since this model is still active, the challenge is therefore not to better inform users or add more tick-boxes. It is to reintroduce governance into the systems themselves: technical governance, organisational governance, and ethical governance.
