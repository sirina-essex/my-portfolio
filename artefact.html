Reflections and case study

Reflective Activity : Ethics in Computing in the age of Generative AI

This reflective analysis explores the ethical and governance challenges raised by the use of generative AI in computing. It critically examines institutional weaknesses, fragmented ethical norms, and the unrealistic expectations placed on individual professionals, drawing on the work of Correa (2023), Deckard (2023), and others to propose a contextualised model of ethical governance.

Connected Medical Implants: Ethics, Vulnerabilities, and Governance Failures

Critical analysis of cybersecurity flaws in medical technologies, based on a case study of Bluetooth-enabled cardiac implants. The discussion applies the ACM and BCS Codes of Ethics to highlight violations of risk governance, lack of transparency, and the ethical consequences of leaving known vulnerabilities uncorrected.

The Inappropriate Use of Surveys: The Cambridge Analytica Case and Compromise Governed by Digital Architecture

Critical discussion of cybersecurity failures, structural governance gaps, and the erosion of individual autonomy in platform-centred data exploitation. Based on the case of Cambridge Analytica, this analysis examines the ethical and legal consequences of designing systems without effective security controls or auditability, explores the systemic absence of transparency and accountability, and applies European data protection frameworks to assess constrained consent, algorithmic opacity, and the societal impact of large-scale psychometric profiling deployed as a tool of political manipulation.

Manipulating Data, Bending Ethics: The Case of Whizzz Cereal

Critical discussion of statistical responsibility and data integrity in industry-funded research. Based on the case of Abi and the Whizzz cereal study, this analysis examines ethical tensions between neutrality and narrative framing, explores the risk of cherry-picking in data reporting, and applies international codes of ethics to assess accountability, whistleblowing duties, and the societal impact of minimising public health risks.

Research Proposal Presentation: Experimental Evaluation of IAM Mechanisms in Azure Under the Zero Trust Model

This presentation summarises the foundations, research question, and methodology of the project entitled “Identity and Access Governance in Microsoft Azure: Experimental Evaluation of IAM Mechanisms for Securing API Access in a Vulnerable Cloud Environment According to Zero Trust Principles.” It highlights the objectives, originality, and applied scope of the study, which focuses on the combined implementation of Azure Entra ID mechanisms (RBAC, PIM, MFA, CA, Access Reviews) in a simulated vulnerable environment (Broken Crystal). The strategy follows a rigorous experimental approach using multi-persona scenarios, targeted attack simulations, log analysis, and cross-validation of data. This document serves as a visual support for the oral defence, aiming to meet the academic expectations of the MSc programme while demonstrating strong applicability to real-world cloud cybersecurity challenges.

Statistical activities

Measures: Descriptive Value and Methodological Limits

This report explores the application of descriptive statistics: including the mean, median, standard deviation, quartiles, and frequencies, to summarise sample data without drawing inferential conclusions. These measures are instrumental in identifying central trends and variability within a dataset. However, their methodological limitations, such as sensitivity to outliers, distortion from skewed distributions, and inability to support hypothesis testing, necessitate a cautious approach to interpretation. The understanding and use of these indicators are informed by classical frameworks described in Field (2013) and Moore et al. (2020), with particular attention to the robustness of the median and the interpretive pitfalls associated with the mean under conditions of dispersion or asymmetry

Hypothesis Testing: Methodological Foundations and Interpretive Boundaries

This analysis applies classical principles of hypothesis testing, including the use of F-tests and t-tests to assess mean differences between groups. The approach is grounded in established statistical literature (Field, 2013; Gravetter & Wallnau, 2016; McClave & Sincich, 2017), with emphasis on the assumptions of independence, normality, and homogeneity of variances. Results are interpreted through the lens of probabilistic inference, recognising the risks of Type I and Type II errors and the inherent uncertainty of p-value-based conclusions. The analysis cautions against overgeneralisation beyond the experimental context and highlights the need to consider potential confounding variables.

Charts: Visual Representation and Perceptual Implications

This report examines the use of bar charts and histograms as visual tools for representing statistical distribution and comparison. These formats enable quick identification of differences, trends, or clusters within a dataset. However, their interpretive value relies on careful construction: axes, scaling, category ordering, and spacing all significantly shape how the viewer perceives the data. Recent literature highlights the impact of graphical choices on data perception, showing that poorly designed visuals may introduce bias or steer interpretation unless methodologically framed. Visualisation should therefore be approached critically, both as a descriptive method and as a cognitive object, an understanding supported by statistical foundations outlined in Moore et al. (2020) and Field (2013), and enriched by the contributions of Mesguich (2021), Andry et al. (2022), and Yau (2013), who examine the intersection between graphical design, perceptual dynamics, and methodological rigour in the context of digital data representation.
