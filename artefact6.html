The Inappropriate Use of Surveys: The Cambridge Analytica Case and Compromise Governed by Digital Architecture

The Cambridge Analytica affair is not an isolated ethical lapse. It is a concrete demonstration of what happens when a digital system is designed without effective governance, without traceability of use, and without any post-collection data security. What the public perceived as a political scandal or a moral failing is, in reality, a structural compromise of the digital interaction model, where the fundamental principles of cybersecurity were ignored from the outset.

Background

In 2014, the Facebook app This Is Your Digital Life enabled a third-party company to harvest the personal data of over 50 million users through default permissions and an open API architecture. None of this data was encrypted, monitored, or even limited in duration or purpose. Hoffman (2022) characterises this mass extraction as an inherent drift of permissive digital architectures, emphasising that the deliberate absence of encryption and oversight constitutes, in itself, a flagrant breach of the security and minimisation requirements imposed by the GDPR (Articles 5 and 32). This was not a traditional attack but an operational hijacking enabled by the lack of technical governance.

A New Threat Model

The compromise unfolded in a coherent sequence: an enticing interface, permissive authorisation, unsupervised extraction, opaque algorithmic processing, and invisible behavioural targeting. Through this process, the data was transformed into psychometric profiles using the OCEAN model, enabling manipulation based on cognitive vulnerabilities (Kosinski, Stillwell and Graepel, 2013). This is no longer advertising—it is large-scale social engineering. Unlike classical cyberattacks, there is no malware, no intrusion, and no malicious code, yet the impact is identical: loss of confidentiality, integrity, and control. Here, the system targeted is the individual.

Constrained Consent

The GDPR relies on free and informed consent. In practice, this protection is undermined by constrained consent: users accept terms by necessity, without comprehension (Solove, 2021). European jurisprudence (CJEU, Planet49, 2019) confirms that such consent often becomes a legal fiction, preserving the asymmetry between users and platforms.

Biometric Data in the FaceApp Case

The 2019 FaceApp affair replicated this model. The app transferred users’ biometric data to servers outside the EU without governance or auditability (O’Flaherty, 2019; Greenleaf, 2018), violating Article 9 of the GDPR. Once captured, such data becomes permanent and unerasable, usable for training facial recognition models.

Systemic Opacity and Architectural Responsibility

In both cases, the problem is architectural. Platforms are designed to extract, aggregate, and derive insights without transparency or traceability. Pasquale (2015) calls this systemic opacity, which undermines individuals’ right—enshrined in Article 22 of the GDPR—to understand and contest automated decisions. Users are embedded in systems they cannot interrogate.

Cybersecurity as a Governance Function

Cybersecurity can no longer be limited to protecting infrastructures. It must become a discipline of governance at the intersection of ethics, law, and structural responsibility. Henderson (2024) argues that professionals should ensure processing integrity, algorithmic accountability, and legitimacy. This role requires moving from passive gatekeeping to active architectural stewardship.

Conclusion

Cambridge Analytica is not an anomaly—it is a consequence of surveillance capitalism (Zuboff, 2019). Regulation remains ineffective if underlying architectures persist. Reintroducing governance—technical, organisational, and ethical—is essential. The most dangerous vulnerabilities lie not in code, but in models. Only cybersecurity as governance can address them.
