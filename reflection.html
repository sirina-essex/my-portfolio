Reflections

I have reflected of what I have learned, unit by unit, in Research Methods.

Foundations of Research and Ethics (Units 1 & 2)

Context: These units introduced the core principles of scientific reasoning and research ethics in computing, focusing on inductive vs. deductive logic, and ethical responsibility in the handling of sensitive data or human involvement.

Actions: I explored the logic behind different reasoning types and examined key ethical frameworks such as the Menlo Report and the BCS Code of Conduct, especially in relation to risk, consent, and transparency.

Result: I gained a clearer understanding of when each reasoning mode is appropriate and how ethics must be embedded throughout the research process, not treated as a final checkpoint.

Learning: This unit reshaped my perception of ethics, not as a constraint, but as a foundation for credible and responsible research. It also helped me revisit professional practices with a more structured and critical mindset.

Methodology and Qualitative Tools (Units 3, 4 & 5)

Learning: Even though methods like focus groups or structured interviews won’t apply directly to my own project, I was still eager to understand how they work. It gave me the opportunity to revisit tools I had encountered professionally and to deepen my foundational knowledge through a more critical and formal lens.

Context: These units examined the link between philosophical paradigms and methodological choices, as well as practical qualitative methods such as case studies, focus groups, observations, and questionnaires.

Actions: I analysed how epistemological and ontological positions guide research design, and critically evaluated various qualitative tools, considering their relevance, biases, and limits in generalisability.

Result: I developed a stronger sense of methodological coherence and was able to clarify where my own project sits, not in direct application of these tools, but informed by their principles of contextual understanding and detailed observation.

Quantitative Methods and Statistical Analysis (Units 6, 7 & 8)

Context: These units focused on the use of descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation), hypothesis testing (t-tests, F-tests), and visualisation tools such as bar charts and histograms, in support of rigorous data interpretation.

Actions: I applied these tools to summarise data and interpret group differences, while also reviewing statistical assumptions (normality, variance homogeneity) and graphical design principles.

Result: I reinforced and extended my understanding of statistical thinking, recognising both the power and the limitations of inference, particularly the risk of misinterpretation or overgeneralisation.

Learning: These units helped me consolidate prior knowledge while developing a more critical awareness of statistical rigour. I now feel more confident navigating data analysis, with a stronger sense of the assumptions and constraints that underpin statistical conclusions.

Validity, Research Writing and Academic Communication (Units 9 & 10)

Learning: This stage helped me shift from a purely technical focus to a more holistic view of research. I now see that clear communication — through writing, structure, and justification — is not secondary but central to academic rigour.

Context: These units addressed core principles of research quality: validity, reliability, generalisability;and introduced best practices in academic writing, particularly for dissertations and structured research proposals.

Actions: I studied how these quality criteria influence design and interpretation, and how academic writing builds a coherent argument through structure, citation, and narrative clarity.

Result: I improved my ability to structure and justify my own research design, while understanding the function of each section within a research report and how it contributes to the overall narrative.

Professional Development and Project Management (Units 11 & 12)

Context: These final units focused on reflective practice and the development of practical project management skills: risk assessment, change control, planning, and portfolio documentation.

Actions: I used tools like risk matrices and lifecycle models to structure my project and applied reflection loops and skills audits to assess my growth and identify areas for further development.

Result: I gained a clearer view of how to manage research as a dynamic process that involves uncertainty, iteration, and strategic control. I also recognised how many of these skills transfer directly to professional practice.

Learning: Bringing together reflection and structured project management gave me a more complete understanding of what it takes to lead a research project with rigour. It also helped me recognise how adaptability, planning, and self-awareness are just as essential as methodological expertise.

Critical Reflection on the Literature Review and Research Proposal

Context: These two assessments were key milestones in my development. The literature review focused on cybersecurity challenges in distributed IoT architectures, while the research proposal explored identity governance in Azure cloud environments. Although the topics were distinct, they both required a structured academic approach and deep engagement with sources.

Result: The transition from the literature review to the research proposal marked a significant improvement in my ability to justify methodological choices and apply academic standards. The second piece received much stronger feedback, especially regarding structure, relevance, and source use. I was able to build on the weaknesses identified in the literature review and turn them into strengths.

Learning: These tasks taught me that research is not just about exploring ideas, but also about making hard decisions, adjusting direction, and maintaining methodological discipline. I realised that writing is not only the outcome of research, it is part of how research takes shape. I also came to appreciate feedback as an essential part of the process, helping me identify blind spots and refine my work.

Actions: The literature review was my first real encounter with synthesising technical content within strict word limits. Given the complexity and interdependence of cybersecurity domains, one of the hardest parts was deciding what to cover and what to leave out. I had to strike a balance between depth and focus. This taught me that good research also means making conscious choices about scope. Feedback highlighted inconsistencies in citation style and occasional lack of source attribution, which I took seriously. In my proposal, I made sure to apply a more consistent referencing style and to support each claim with properly cited evidence. Building the proposal required refining my initial topic, which turned out to be too close to existing work. I had to reframe my focus and develop a clearer, more original angle. This process included selecting a viable research method, structuring a realistic plan, and aligning each component with a clearly defined problem and question.
