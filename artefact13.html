Comparison of Average Weight Loss Between Diet A and Diet B

Context and Objective

This analysis compares the average weight loss between two groups of 50 independent participants, each following a different diet (Diet A and Diet B). Each participant was assigned to only one diet, making the samples independent. The goal is to assess whether one diet is statistically more effective than the other in promoting weight loss.

Preliminary Step : F-Test for Equality of Variances

MeasureValue
Variance - Diet A6.429
Variance - Diet B7.668
F Statistic0.839
p-value (two-tailed)0.5399

Interpretation: Since the p-value from the F-test (0.5399) is greater than the significance threshold of 0.05, we fail to reject the null hypothesis of equal variances. It is therefore appropriate to proceed with an independent samples t-test assuming equal variances.

t-Test for Independent Means

MeasureValue
Mean - Diet A5.341 kg
Mean - Diet B3.710 kg
Observed Difference+1.631 kg
t Statistic3.072
Degrees of Freedom98
p-value (two-tailed)0.00275

Interpretation

The p-value of 0.00275 is well below the standard significance level of 0.05, leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis of equal means. The observed average weight loss is 1.631 kg higher for Diet A than Diet B, and this difference is statistically significant, suggesting that Diet A is more effective within the studied sample.

Methodological Assumptions

The independent t-test with equal variances relies on the following assumptions:

  • Independence: Each participant is part of only one group, with no overlap.
  • Normality: The distribution of weight loss should be approximately normal within each group. Given n = 50 per group, the t-test is robust to moderate departures from normality.
  • Equality of variances: Confirmed by the F-test (p = 0.5399).

Conclusion

The statistical analysis demonstrates that Diet A results in significantly greater average weight loss than Diet B, with a mean difference of approximately 1.63 kg and a highly significant p-value of 0.00275. This suggests that the likelihood of observing such a difference due to chance is low if the diets were actually equivalent.

However, the result remains probabilistic: there is still a small Type I error risk (≈ 0.27%), meaning there is a slight chance the conclusion is incorrect. Conversely, in smaller or poorly powered samples, a Type II error could occur, failing to detect a real difference. Finally, while assumptions have been met, generalisation beyond the experimental context should be made cautiously, particularly considering possible confounding factors like physical activity, metabolism, or diet adherence.

Comparison of Impurity Levels Between Two Filtration Agents

Paired Sample t-Test Results

MeasureValue
Mean - Agent 18.25

Mean Difference-0.43
t Statistic-3.264
Degrees of Freedom11
Two-tailed p-value0.0075

Interpretation: The p-value of 0.0075 is well below the conventional significance threshold of α = 0.05, leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis. This indicates a statistically significant difference between the two filtration agents. The negative sign of the mean difference (-0.43) and the t-statistic suggests that Agent 1 achieves a more effective reduction in impurities. These results point to superior performance by Agent 1 under the study conditions.

Methodology and Assumptions: A paired samples t-test was used, which is appropriate given the structure of the dataset. This test relies on two main assumptions: the independence of pairs—ensured by the experimental protocol, and the normality of the differences between pairs, specifically the distribution of differences in impurity levels between the two methods.

Assumption Validation:The 12 pairs derive from distinct product batches, satisfying the independence criterion. The assumption of normality, though not formally tested here, may be assessed using a histogram or a Shapiro-Wilk test. Given the relatively small sample size (n = 12), caution is warranted; however, the t-test is considered robust to moderate deviations from normality, provided no extreme outliers are present.

Conclusion: The analysis shows that Agent 1 significantly reduces impurities more than Agent 2, with a mean difference of 0.43 units and a p-value of 0.0075. This result is statistically strong and suggests a genuine advantage for Agent 1. However, as with all inferential conclusions, the outcome remains probabilistic: the type I error risk is below 1%, but not entirely eliminated. Given the limited sample size, contextual factors could still influence the findings.

One-Tailed Test: Is Agent 1 More Effective than Agent 2?

Paired Sample t-Test Results (One-Tailed)

MeasureValue
Mean - Agent 18.25
Mean - Agent 28.68
Mean Difference-0.43
t Statistic-3.264
Degrees of Freedom11
One-tailed p-value0.0038

Interpretation: The one-tailed p-value of 0.0038, well below the 0.05 threshold, leads to the rejection of the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative. This suggests that Filtration Agent 1 significantly reduces impurity levels more than Agent 2. The negative sign of both the t-statistic and the mean difference (-0.43) confirms this trend. The risk of a type I error—estimated here at 0.38%—remains low, though not entirely negligible.

Assumptions and Test Validity:The test assumes independence of paired samples, which is ensured by the experimental design, and normality of the differences between observations. Although not formally tested here, the normality assumption is considered reasonable for 12 pairs in the absence of outliers. The paired t-test is generally robust in such conditions.

Conclusion: This one-tailed test provides strong statistical evidence in favour of Agent 1's superior performance, with a significant mean difference and a p-value well below the α threshold. Nonetheless, this conclusion remains probabilistic: there is always a small residual risk of error. Rejecting the null hypothesis significantly reduces—but does not eliminate—this uncertainty.

Ethical and Methodological Note: To preserve statistical integrity, the choice of a one-tailed test must be defined before any data analysis. Selecting it post hoc, based on observed trends, would invalidate the test and artificially inflate significance. In this case, the directional hypothesis was established a priori, ensuring the methodological rigour of the analysis.

MeasureValue

Observed Difference£8.680k
t Statistic3.268
Degrees of Freedom118
One-tailed p-value0.00071

Interpretation:The one-tailed p-value obtained (0.00071) is well below the standard significance threshold of 0.05, allowing us to reject the null hypothesis. This indicates that, on average, men have significantly higher income than women within this sample. The observed mean difference is £8.680k.

Assumptions and Test Validity:The validity of the applied t-test relies on three assumptions: independence between the two groups (men and women), assumed normality of income distributions (reasonable given the sample size, n = 60), and equality of variances, confirmed by a preliminary F-test. Under these conditions, the t-test is considered robust.

Conclusion: The statistical analysis reveals a significant income difference in favour of men among Superplus Diamond cardholders. The risk of a type I error (wrongly concluding there is a difference) is below 0.1%, which strengthens the credibility of the result. However, this conclusion is probabilistic: it suggests a strong likelihood of a genuine difference in means, not an absolute certainty.

Warning on Generalisation: These findings should not be generalised without caution. Factors such as age, occupation, or card tenure may also influence income but were not accounted for. Moreover, the one-tailed test was selected a priori, in line with good inferential practice. A post hoc choice would have undermined the validity of the statistical significance observed.
