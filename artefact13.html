Hypothesis Testing: Statistical Significance and Inference in Paired and Independent Sample Comparisons

Product Sales Comparison Between Two Packaging Designs

MeasureValue
Mean - Design 1172.6 units
Mean - Design 2159.4 units
Difference+13.2 units
t Statistic2.875
Degrees of Freedom9
Two-tailed p-value0.0183
One-tailed p-value0.0092

Interpretation: The average number of units sold was significantly higher for Design 1. The p-value (0.0183) is below the α = 0.05 threshold, allowing us to reject the null hypothesis. Design 1 appears more effective in increasing sales.

Methodology: A paired samples t-test was used due to the matched design. The assumptions of independence and normality of differences were reasonably satisfied, and the test is robust for n = 10.

Conclusion: There is statistical evidence supporting the superiority of Design 1 in this sample. However, the finding remains probabilistic and subject to Type I error risk.

Comparison of Impurity Levels Between Two Filtration Agents

Paired Sample t-Test Results

MeasureValue
Mean - Agent 18.25
Mean - Agent 28.68
Mean Difference-0.43
t Statistic-3.264
Degrees of Freedom11
Two-tailed p-value0.0075

Interpretation: The p-value (0.0075) is well below the conventional α = 0.05 threshold, and even below 0.01. We therefore reject the null hypothesis. The results indicate a statistically significant difference in mean impurity levels between the two agents. The negative mean difference and t statistic show that Agent 1 produces lower impurity levels, suggesting better filtration performance.

Methodology: A paired samples t-test was used, as each pair of values corresponds to the same batch filtered through two different agents. The assumptions for this test include: (1) independence of the 12 batches (confirmed by design), and (2) approximate normality of the differences. Although the sample size is small (n = 12), the test is robust against moderate deviations from normality provided no strong outliers are present. Normality was assumed reasonable for this exercise. A prior visual inspection (e.g., Q-Q plot or histogram) or Shapiro-Wilk test would strengthen this assumption.

Conclusion: Agent 1 appears significantly more effective than Agent 2 in reducing impurity levels. The difference is statistically supported (p = 0.0075), with low risk of Type I error. However, due to the small sample size, generalisation beyond this sample must remain cautious. These findings are probabilistic and not definitive: the low p-value suggests strong evidence, not absolute certainty.

One-Tailed Test: Is Agent 1 More Effective than Agent 2?

Paired Sample t-Test Results (One-Tailed)

MeasureValue
Mean - Agent 18.25
Mean - Agent 28.68
Mean Difference-0.43
t Statistic-3.264
Degrees of Freedom11
One-tailed p-value0.0038

Interpretation: The one-tailed p-value (0.0038) is well below the standard significance threshold of α = 0.05, allowing us to reject the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative. This supports the claim that Agent 1 is more effective than Agent 2, producing fewer residual impurities on average. The negative mean difference and t statistic confirm the expected direction of the effect.

Methodology: A one-tailed paired t-test was used, based on the experimental setup where each product batch was divided and filtered using both agents. The directional hypothesis (Agent 1 < Agent 2) was specified a priori, which is essential to maintain statistical integrity and avoid confirmation bias.

Conclusion: Agent 1 appears significantly more effective in reducing impurities, with strong statistical evidence (p = 0.0038) and low probability of a Type I error (less than 0.4%). Nonetheless, this conclusion is conditional on the assumptions being met:

  • Independence: Met by experimental design.
  • Normality of differences: Assumed reasonable for n = 12; graphical checks are recommended for full validation.

The t-test remains robust to moderate violations of normality, provided there are no strong outliers. These findings are probabilistic, not definitive: the p-value indicates strong support but not certainty.

Ethical and Methodological Note: It is crucial that the one-tailed nature of the test was established before viewing the data. Choosing a one-tailed test after seeing a favourable result would invalidate the inferential process and artificially increase statistical significance.

Independent Samples t-Test (One-Tailed, Equal Variances)

MeasureValue
Mean - Men£52.913k
Mean - Women£44.233k
Observed Difference£8.680k
t Statistic3.268
Degrees of Freedom118
One-tailed p-value0.00071

Interpretation: The one-tailed p-value of 0.00071 is well below the α = 0.05 threshold, allowing us to reject the null hypothesis. This indicates a statistically significant difference in favour of men, who earn on average £8.680k more than women in this sample.

Methodology: The one-tailed independent samples t-test with equal variances was chosen based on an a priori research question: whether men earn more than women. The assumptions for the test are as follows:

  • Independence: Satisfied - two distinct groups.
  • Normality: Assumed reasonable; n = 60 per group → test is robust.
  • Equal variances: Confirmed by F-test (p = 0.218).

Conclusion: The analysis reveals a statistically significant income gap in favour of men among Superplus Diamond cardholders. The one-tailed p-value (0.00071) implies a Type I error risk below 0.1%, lending strong credibility to the finding. However, this conclusion is probabilistic, not absolute. It indicates that, on average, the difference is unlikely to have arisen by chance if there were no real income disparity.

Warning on Generalisation: This conclusion:

  • Should not be generalised to all customers or all card types.
  • Does not account for possible confounding variables (e.g. age, occupation, card tenure).
  • Relies on an a priori decision to use a one-tailed test, not adjusted post hoc based on observed outcomes.

A one-tailed test chosen after seeing the data would violate inferential principles and artificially inflate significance.
